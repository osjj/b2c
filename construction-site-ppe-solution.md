Construction is the most dangerous private industry in the United States. In 2023, the sector recorded 1,075 fatalities — the highest number since 2011 — accounting for roughly one in five of all workplace deaths that year. Behind every one of those numbers is a worker who went to a job site and did not come home.

The right PPE, properly selected, properly fitted, and consistently worn, cannot eliminate every hazard on a construction site. But it is the final, critical barrier between a worker and serious injury when everything else — engineering controls, administrative procedures, training — has not been enough.

This guide covers everything construction site managers, safety officers, and procurement teams need to know about equipping a workforce with the correct personal protective equipment: which hazards demand which gear, how OSHA and EN/CE requirements affect selection, how to select certified equipment for each risk category, and how to manage PPE at scale across a construction operation.

Whether your sites operate under OSHA (US), EU Regulation 2016/425 (Europe), or you source globally and need dual-certified equipment, this guide provides the complete reference.

## The Construction Hazard Landscape: What Your Workers Face Every Day

OSHA has identified four categories of hazard — known as the **Fatal Four** — that together account for more than 60% of all construction worker deaths every year. Understanding these hazards is the foundation of any effective PPE strategy.

### Falls (38.5% of construction fatalities)

Falls are the leading cause of death in construction by a wide margin. In 2023, falls, slips, and trips caused 421 construction fatalities — 38.5% of all deaths in the sector. The construction industry accounted for 47.8% of all fatal falls across every U.S. industry combined. The majority of fatal falls — 64.4% — occurred from heights between 6 and 30 feet. Roofing contractors alone accounted for 26% of construction fall fatalities.

Fall hazards arise from unprotected edges and floor openings, improper scaffolding, ladder misuse, and the absence of personal fall arrest systems. Fall protection has been OSHA's most frequently cited construction standard for 14 consecutive years.

### Struck-by incidents (second leading cause)

Struck-by injuries occur when a worker is hit by a flying, falling, swinging, or rolling object. Common causes include falling tools and materials from elevated work areas, swinging crane loads, moving vehicles and heavy equipment on site, and nail gun discharges. Hard hats, high-visibility clothing, and eye protection are the primary PPE defenses against struck-by hazards.

### Caught-in/between incidents

Workers can be caught, crushed, or pinched between machinery and fixed objects, between moving equipment and walls, or buried in trench cave-ins. Appropriate gloves, body-fitting clothing that cannot snag on moving parts, and proper machine guarding are the relevant PPE considerations.

### Electrocution (significant contributor to fatalities)

Electrical hazards include contact with overhead power lines, damaged or live wires, exposed wiring, and improperly grounded equipment. Electricians and construction laborers are among the occupational groups most frequently killed by electrocution. Insulated gloves, dielectric footwear, arc-flash rated clothing, and non-conductive hard hats are required for electrical work.

### Additional hazards beyond the Fatal Four

Beyond the Fatal Four, construction workers face a range of additional hazards that PPE must address:

**Respiratory hazards** are pervasive on construction sites. Concrete cutting, drilling, and demolition generate respirable crystalline silica — a Group 1 carcinogen. Demolition of older structures may release asbestos. Welding and grinding produce metal fumes. Painting and chemical application generates solvent vapors.

**Noise** is one of the most consistently under-addressed hazards in construction. Long-term exposure to equipment noise above 85 dBA causes permanent hearing loss. Tinnitus — persistent ringing in the ears — affects more than 7% of construction workers, making it one of the most common occupational conditions in the industry.

**Chemical and dermal hazards** include cement and concrete contact dermatitis, adhesive and sealant chemical exposure, and UV radiation for outdoor workers.

## OSHA PPE Requirements for Construction: What the Law Requires

### The core standard: 29 CFR 1926 Subpart E

OSHA's construction PPE requirements are governed primarily by 29 CFR 1926 Subpart E, with the master criteria set out in 29 CFR 1926.95. Under this standard, employers must:

- Conduct a workplace hazard assessment to identify PPE requirements
- Provide appropriate PPE to all workers exposed to identified hazards, at no cost to the employee in most circumstances
- Ensure PPE is maintained in a sanitary and reliable condition
- Train workers on the proper use, fit, and care of all PPE they are required to wear

### The 2025 proper-fit rule: what changed

Effective **January 13, 2025**, OSHA finalized an important revision to 29 CFR 1926.95(c). The updated standard now explicitly states that employers must ensure all PPE "is selected to ensure that it properly fits each affected employee."

This change aligns construction with OSHA's long-standing general industry standard and has direct operational implications. PPE that does not fit properly can fail to protect — a harness that is too large may not arrest a fall correctly; goggles that gap at the face leave eyes exposed; gloves that are too long can get caught in moving machinery and cause the injuries they are supposed to prevent. The rule also covers workers who provide their own PPE: employers must verify that employee-supplied equipment both meets safety standards and fits correctly.

As of the federal adjustment effective **January 15, 2025**, OSHA penalties can reach **$16,550** for serious violations and **$165,514** for willful or repeated violations. If you need the regulation-first version of this topic, read [OSHA PPE requirements for construction: compliance guide](/blog/osha-ppe-requirements-construction).

## Complete Construction PPE: Category-by-Category Guide

### 1. Fall Protection

Fall protection is required on construction sites whenever workers are at heights of **6 feet or more** above a lower level, or working over dangerous machinery or equipment at any height (29 CFR 1926.501).

**Personal Fall Arrest Systems (PFAS)** are the primary PPE component for fall protection. A complete PFAS consists of:

- **Full-body harness** — distributes fall-arrest forces across the thighs, pelvis, chest, and shoulders. Must meet ANSI Z359.11 (US) or **EN 361** (Europe). EN 361 requires the harness to withstand a static force of 15 kN and pass a dynamic drop test using a 100 kg dummy with a 4-metre free fall. Under the 2025 OSHA update, harnesses must be selected in the correct size for each worker — not simply adjusted to fit.
- **Shock-absorbing lanyard or self-retracting lifeline (SRL)** — connects the harness to the anchor point. Shock-absorbing lanyards (US: ANSI Z359.13 / EU: **EN 354 + EN 355**) extend to a maximum of 6 feet (1.8 m) and reduce peak arrest forces. EN 354 lanyards must withstand a minimum static force of 22 kN. SRLs (EU: **EN 360**) allow greater freedom of movement and deploy automatically when a fall begins; EN 360 requires the braking force not to exceed 6 kN with a maximum arrest distance of 2 metres.
- **Anchor point** — must support at least 5,000 pounds (22 kN) per attached worker (US: ANSI Z359.15 / EU: **EN 795**). EN 795 classifies anchor devices by type (A through E) depending on whether they are fixed structural anchors, movable anchors, or horizontal lifelines.

**European fall protection system standards:**
- **EN 363** — governs complete fall protection systems, covering restraint, work positioning, rope access, fall arrest, and rescue systems as integrated assemblies
- **EN 358** — work positioning belts and lanyards for workers who need to be held in position while working at height (distinct from fall arrest)
- **EN 813** — sit harnesses for suspension work

**Other fall protection options** that may substitute for or supplement PFAS:
- Guardrails (US: top rail at 42 inches ±3 inches / EU: minimum 1 metre per EN 13374)
- Safety nets (US: 6×6 inch max mesh / EU: EN 1263-1)
- Warning lines and safety monitors (limited to specific roofing applications)

**Inspection requirements:** All PFAS components must be inspected before each use. Under both OSHA and EN standards, any equipment that has arrested a fall must be immediately removed from service — the internal structure may be compromised even if no visible damage is present.

### 2. Head Protection

Hard hats are mandatory wherever there is a potential for injury from falling objects, bumping the head against fixed objects, or contact with electrical conductors (29 CFR 1926.100 in the US; **EU Regulation 2016/425** and **EN 397** in Europe).

**ANSI/ISEA Z89.1 classifications (US):**

**By type:**
- **Type I** — protects the top of the head only. Standard for most US construction applications.
- **Type II** — protects the top and sides of the head. Increasingly required as OSHA and industry standards evolve. Recommended for any work involving lateral impact risks such as scaffolding, steel erection, and work near moving equipment.

**By electrical class:**
- **Class E (Electrical)** — rated for up to 20,000 volts. Required for electrical work.
- **Class G (General)** — rated for up to 2,200 volts. Suitable for general construction.
- **Class C (Conductive)** — no electrical protection. Not appropriate where electrical hazards are present.

**EN 397 (European standard):**

EN 397 is the primary European standard for industrial safety helmets. All EN 397 helmets must pass a set of mandatory tests:
- **Impact absorption** — tested by dropping a 5 kg striker onto the helmet from 1 metre height; the force transmitted to a headform must not exceed 5 kN
- **Penetration resistance** — a pointed striker dropped from 1 metre must not contact the headform
- **Flame resistance** — the helmet material must not continue to burn for more than 5 seconds after removal of the flame
- **Chin strap retention** — straps must release at a force between 150 N and 250 N to prevent strangulation in the event of a snag

EN 397 also includes a set of **optional tested properties** that may be marked on the helmet:
- **-20°C or -30°C** — cold temperature performance (important for winter construction in northern climates)
- **+150°C** — molten metal splash and radiant heat resistance (for foundry and hot-works applications)
- **440 V ac** — electrical insulation (equivalent to Class G; if 440V ac is marked, the helmet provides basic electrical protection)
- **LD** — lateral deformation resistance (important for scaffolding and steel erection where side impacts are possible)
- **MM** — molten metal splash resistance

**EN 397 vs ANSI Z89.1:** Both standards provide broadly equivalent head impact protection for the top of the head. The key difference is that ANSI Type II explicitly addresses lateral (side) impact — currently not a mandatory element of EN 397, though EN 12492 (mountaineering helmets) and certain newer helmet designs do address this. For maximum protection on European sites, look for helmets carrying both the **EN 397** mark and the optional **LD** (lateral deformation) marking.

**Replacement and inspection:** Hard hats must be replaced if they show cracks, dents, or penetration marks, after any significant impact, and according to manufacturer service life recommendations (typically 5 years for the shell, 1–2 years for the suspension). Under EN 397, helmets must be marked with their date of manufacture — check this when receiving bulk orders.

### 3. High-Visibility Clothing

High-visibility (hi-vis) clothing protects workers from struck-by hazards involving vehicles and moving equipment. It is required wherever workers are exposed to vehicular traffic or heavy equipment movement (29 CFR 1926.201 for flaggers in the US; **EN ISO 20471** in Europe, mandated under EU Regulation 2016/425).

**ANSI/ISEA 107 performance classes (US):**

- **Class 1** — minimum visibility for low-risk environments with low vehicle speeds. Not appropriate for most active construction sites.
- **Class 2** — the standard minimum for most US construction work. Vests, jackets, or shirts with both fluorescent background material and retroreflective striping. Required for most workers on or near vehicle traffic.
- **Class 3** — maximum visibility for the highest-risk environments: road construction, highway work, and any site with high-speed vehicle movement. Full coverage of torso, arms, and legs with retroreflective material.

**EN ISO 20471 (European standard):**

EN ISO 20471 is the primary European standard for high-visibility clothing, structured around three classes based on the minimum area of fluorescent background material and retroreflective material:

| EN ISO 20471 Class | Min. fluorescent background area | Min. retroreflective area | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Class 1 | 0.14 m² | 0.10 m² | Low-risk environments, slow traffic |
| Class 2 | 0.50 m² | 0.13 m² | Most construction sites, moderate-risk traffic |
| Class 3 | 0.80 m² | 0.20 m² | Road construction, high-speed traffic |

**ANSI 107 vs EN ISO 20471:** Both standards use a three-class system with broadly similar intent. Class 3 under both standards represents the highest protection level. Key differences include specific measurement methods and the fact that EN ISO 20471 Class 3 requires coverage of both the torso and limbs (not just a vest), making it the stricter requirement for the highest-risk environments. For global procurement, garments certified to **EN ISO 20471 Class 3** will satisfy both European requirements and the intent of ANSI 107 Class 3.

**Material considerations:** Fluorescent yellow-green offers the highest daytime visibility under both standards. Orange provides good contrast in some environments. Both colors must meet minimum retroreflective requirements for nighttime and low-light conditions. Where work involves welding or open flame, flame-resistant hi-vis clothing certified to both **EN ISO 20471** and **EN ISO 11612** (flame protection) is required.

### 4. Eye and Face Protection

Eye and face protection is required whenever workers are exposed to flying particles, molten metal, liquid chemicals, acids, caustic liquids, chemical gases, or light radiation (29 CFR 1926.102 in the US; **EN 166** in Europe). All eye and face protection must meet ANSI/ISEA Z87.1 (US) or **EN 166** (EU).

**EN 166 marking system:** European eye protection carries a code stamped on the lens and frame that communicates its protective properties. Key markings include:

- **1** — optical class (1 = suitable for continuous use with minimal optical distortion; 3 = for occasional use only)
- **2** — liquid droplets and splashes protection
- **3** — large dust particles (>5 microns)
- **4** — gas and fine dust particles
- **5** — arc flash and short-circuit electric arc (used with **EN 379** filter lenses)
- **BT** — fine particles AND gas protection combined

Frame code markings on EN 166 equipment:
- **3** — adjustable temples (for hard hat compatibility)
- **4** — headband
- **5** — reduced face contact (for use with respiratory protection)

**Selection guide by task:**

- **General construction, grinding, chipping, nailing** — safety glasses with side protection (ANSI Z87.1 / EN 166). Impact-rated polycarbonate lenses. Look for **EN 166 marking "3"** (large dust) for dusty environments.
- **Concrete work, chemical exposure, dust** — indirect-vent safety goggles (EN 166 **"3 4"** marking) providing a sealed barrier against particles and splashes.
- **Welding (stick, MIG, TIG)** — welding helmet with appropriate shade lens. US: shade 9–13 per ANSI Z87.1; EU: **EN 175** for welding helmets, with filter shade number per **EN 169**.
- **Grinding and cutting near welders** — shade 3–5 filter goggles (**EN 166 + EN 170/172**).
- **Chemical handling, adhesives, sealants** — chemical splash goggles (EN 166 **"2"** liquid droplet marking).
- **Heavy debris risk (demolition, chipping)** — face shield over safety glasses (EU: **EN 166** for the goggle/glasses + face shield tested to **EN 166 marking "B"** for medium energy impact).

### 5. Hearing Protection

Construction workers are exposed to some of the highest sustained noise levels of any industry. Jackhammers operate at around 130 dB; power saws at 110 dB; pneumatic drills at 100 dB. OSHA requires hearing protection when noise exposure reaches or exceeds 90 dBA as an 8-hour time-weighted average (29 CFR 1926.52). In Europe, the EU Physical Agents (Noise) Directive 2003/10/EC requires hearing protection at 85 dB(A) as an 8-hour daily exposure, and mandates it at 87 dB(A).

For task-level selection, NRR/SNR comparison, fit, compatibility, and replacement planning, use the [construction hearing protection guide](/blog/construction-hearing-protection).

**US standard — Noise Reduction Rating (NRR):** All US hearing protectors carry an NRR label under ANSI S3.19. When calculating real-world protection, divide the NRR by 2 and subtract from the ambient noise level. For example, an NRR 30 protector provides approximately 15 dB of real-world attenuation.

**European standard — EN 352 series and SNR rating:** European hearing protection is governed by the **EN 352** standard series:
- **EN 352-1** — earmuffs
- **EN 352-2** — earplugs
- **EN 352-3** — earmuffs attached to industrial safety helmets (critical for construction workers who wear hard hats)
- **EN 352-4** — level-dependent earmuffs (allow normal hearing at low noise, attenuate at high noise)
- **EN 352-8** — hearing protectors with entertainment audio (for long-duration wear)

European protectors use the **SNR (Single Number Rating)** system rather than NRR. SNR represents the attenuation in dB at the most common workplace noise frequencies. To estimate protected exposure under the SNR system: subtract SNR from the C-weighted ambient noise level (dB(C)). A protector with SNR 28 in a 105 dB(C) environment yields approximately 77 dB(A) protected exposure.

**Practical selection guide:**

- **Disposable foam earplugs** (NRR 25–33 / SNR 20–35): highest attenuation for the cost. Best for consistent high-noise environments. Must be correctly inserted — incorrect insertion dramatically reduces actual protection.
- **Reusable banded earplugs**: convenient for workers who move frequently in and out of high-noise areas. NRR/SNR typically lower than foam earplugs.
- **Earmuffs** (NRR 20–30 / SNR 25–33): more consistent protection. Critical: for hard hat compatibility, select earmuffs certified to **EN 352-3** that are specifically designed to attach to EN 397 or ANSI Z89.1 helmets without compromising the helmet's protective properties.
- **Dual protection** (earplugs + earmuffs): required when noise exceeds 105 dBA (US) or 105 dB(A) (EU).

### 6. Respiratory Protection

Construction sites generate a range of airborne hazards that respiratory PPE must address. A single site may expose workers to silica dust from concrete cutting, asbestos from demolition, organic solvent vapors from adhesives and coatings, and welding fumes — sometimes simultaneously.

**US vs European respiratory protection ratings:**

| US (NIOSH) | European (EN 149) | Filtration efficiency | Typical construction use |
|---|---|---|---|
| N95 | FFP2 | ≥94–95% | General construction dust, low-silica tasks |
| N99 | FFP2 (with better fit) | ≥99% | — |
| P100 / N100 | FFP3 | ≥99–99.97% | Silica, asbestos, welding fumes, demolition |

Key difference: NIOSH P100 filters 99.97% of particles and is oil-proof. EN 149 FFP3 filters a minimum of 99% of particles. For the highest-risk construction tasks — silica from jackhammering, asbestos from demolition — **FFP3 / P100** is the minimum acceptable level of respiratory protection.

EN 149 respirators also carry a **reuse marking**:
- **NR (Not Reusable)** — single-use only, must be disposed of after each shift
- **R (Reusable)** — can be used for more than one shift; must be stored hygienically between uses
- **D** — indicates the respirator has passed a dolomite dust clogging test, confirming performance in dusty environments over time. For construction, look for FFP3 **NR D** or **R D** respirators.

**Respiratory PPE by construction hazard:**

- **Silica dust** (concrete cutting, drilling, grinding, demolition): minimum **N95/FFP2** for incidental or low-exposure tasks; **P100/FFP3** half-face respirator for regular or heavy exposure. OSHA's silica PEL is 50 µg/m³ as an 8-hour TWA (29 CFR 1926.1153); EU OEL is 0.1 mg/m³ respirable crystalline silica.
- **General construction dust** (wood, drywall, general particulates): **N95/FFP2** minimum.
- **Asbestos** (demolition of pre-1980 buildings): half-face respirator with **P100/FFP3** filters as a minimum; full-face PAPR for high-disturbance activities. Both OSHA (29 CFR 1926.1101) and the EU Asbestos Directive (2023/2668/EU) require a full respiratory protection program.
- **Organic vapor hazards** (adhesives, sealants, epoxies, paints): half-face or full-face respirator with **OV/P100** (US) or **A2P3** (EU, per EN 14387) combination cartridges.
- **Spray painting and coating**: full-face respirator with OV/P100 or A2P3 cartridges, or supplied-air respirator for high-volume applications.

All respiratory protective equipment must be NIOSH-approved (US) or CE-marked to EN 149 / EN 140 / EN 136 (EU). A complete respiratory protection program — including hazard assessment, respirator selection, fit testing, and worker training — is required under 29 CFR 1910.134 (US) and **EN 529** (EU guidance on RPE programme management).

### 7. Hand Protection

Hands are among the most frequently injured body parts in construction. Cut lacerations, crush injuries, chemical burns, vibration damage, and thermal injuries all require different protective solutions. OSHA requires appropriate hand protection whenever workers' hands are exposed to hazards (29 CFR 1926.28 / EU: **EN 388** for mechanical risks, **EN 374** for chemical risks, **EN 407** for thermal risks).

**EN 388 — Mechanical hazard gloves:**

EN 388 is the primary European standard for protective gloves against mechanical hazards. The standard uses a four-performance code (plus two additional characters in the 2016 revision) stamped on the glove:

**Position 1 — Abrasion resistance** (levels 1–4, or X if not tested; 4 = highest)  
**Position 2 — Cut resistance (Coupe blade test)** (levels 1–5, or X; 5 = highest)  
**Position 3 — Tear resistance** (levels 1–4; 4 = highest)  
**Position 4 — Puncture resistance** (levels 1–4; 4 = highest)  
**Position 5 — Cut resistance (ISO 13997/TDM test)** (levels A–F; F = highest; used when blade test is not applicable at cut level 5)  
**Position 6 — Impact protection** (P = passed; used for gloves with dorsal knuckle protection)

Example: A glove marked **EN 388: 4X43BP** offers maximum abrasion resistance, blade cut not applicable (TDM test used), tear level 4, puncture level 3, TDM cut level B, and passed impact test.

**EN 388 vs ANSI/ISEA 105 cut levels:**

| EN 388 TDM level | ANSI/ISEA 105 cut level | Min. force to cut through |
|---|---|---|
| A | A1 | 200–499 g |
| B | A2–A3 | 500–999 g |
| C | A4 | 1,000–1,499 g |
| D | A5 | 1,500–2,199 g |
| E | A6 | 2,200–2,999 g |
| F | A7–A9 | ≥3,000 g |

**Glove selection by construction task:**

- **General construction, handling materials** — leather palm or synthetic leather work gloves; EN 388 minimum **2X23** or ANSI A2
- **Concrete and masonry work** — nitrile or neoprene-coated gloves (**EN 374** chemical resistance); extended cuffs to keep cement from entering at the wrist. Cement causes severe skin burns — look for gloves tested for alkaline chemical resistance.
- **Grinding, metal cutting, sheet metal work** — EN 388 cut level D–F / ANSI A4–A6; dorsal impact protection (Position 6 = P) strongly recommended
- **Electrical work** — voltage-rated insulating rubber gloves to **EN 60903** (EU equivalent of ASTM D120); Classes 00 to 4 by voltage rating; must be worn over leather protector gloves
- **Chemical handling** — gloves selected per **EN 374-1** and EN 374-2/3 matched to the specific chemical. Look for the "Type B" or "Type A" marking (Type A = highest chemical permeation resistance across 6 chemicals)
- **Vibrating tools** (jackhammers, compactors, grinders) — anti-vibration gloves to **EN ISO 10819**; reduces hand-arm vibration exposure to help prevent HAVS (Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome)

### 8. Safety Footwear

Construction workers face foot hazards from falling objects, punctures from nails and sharp debris, slips on wet or uneven surfaces, and compression from heavy equipment. Safety footwear requirements are set out at 29 CFR 1926.96 (US, ASTM F2413) and under **EN ISO 20345:2022+A1:2024** in Europe.

**ASTM F2413 rating components (US):**

- **Impact resistance (I/75 or I/50)** — protective toe cap tested to resist a 75 ft-lb or 50 ft-lb impact
- **Compression resistance (C/75 or C/50)** — protective toe tested against 2,500 lb or 1,750 lb compressive load
- **Metatarsal protection (Mt)** — protects the bones on the top of the foot from crushing impacts
- **Puncture resistance (PR)** — midsole protection against nail penetration
- **Electrical hazard rating (EH)** — insulating outsole for protection against live electrical circuits
- **Slip resistance (SR)** — tested slip resistance on wet and oily surfaces

**EN ISO 20345:2022+A1:2024 — S-class system (Europe):**

EN ISO 20345 requires all safety footwear to provide a 200-joule impact-resistant toe cap (compared to ASTM's 75 ft-lb / ~102 joule standard). Beyond this baseline, footwear is classified by "S" rating:

| EN ISO 20345 class | Toe cap (200J) | Closed heel | Antistatic | Heel energy absorption | Water-resistant upper (WPA) | Puncture-resistant midsole | Cleated outsole | Fully waterproof |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SB** | ✓ | Optional | — | — | — | — | — | — |
| **S1** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | — | — | — |
| **S1P** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | ✓ | — | — |
| **S2** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | — | — |
| **S3** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| **S4** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | — | — | ✓ (rubber/polymer) |
| **S5** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | — | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (rubber/polymer) |

**Construction recommendation:** For most outdoor construction work, **S3** is the standard minimum in Europe — it provides puncture resistance, water-resistant upper, and a cleated outsole for uneven and wet terrain. For electrical workers, S1P with an **ESD** (electrostatic dissipative) or **EH** marking is required.

**Additional EN ISO 20345 markings relevant to construction:**

- **HI** — heat insulation: outsole resists contact with surfaces heated to 150°C (relevant for road surfacing, hot-works)
- **CI** — cold insulation: insulation for work in cold environments down to -17°C (relevant for winter construction in northern climates)
- **LG** — ladder grip: new in the 2022 update; certifies that the outsole provides secure grip on ladder rungs — particularly important for scaffolding workers and roofers
- **SRC / SR** — slip resistance on ceramic tile with detergent and glycerine (note: under the 2022 standard, "SRA/SRB/SRC" designations are replaced by a unified "SR" marking)
- **WR** — fully waterproof footwear (tested as a complete shoe, not just the upper)

**ASTM F2413 vs EN ISO 20345:** Both standards provide equivalent toe cap protection. Key practical differences: EN ISO 20345 requires 200 joules vs ASTM's ~102 joule equivalent; EN ISO 20345 S3 automatically bundles puncture resistance with a cleated outsole; and the new 2022 standard adds the LG (ladder grip) marking with no direct ASTM equivalent. For globally sourced PPE programs, footwear certified to EN ISO 20345 S3 SR generally meets or exceeds ASTM F2413 requirements for most construction applications.

**Transition note:** New products can be certified under the old EN ISO 20345:2011 standard until November 2024; existing certificates remain valid until their expiry. Both the 2011 and 2022 standard products will coexist in the market until approximately 2029.

### 9. Electrical PPE

For workers performing electrical work or working near live conductors, standard construction PPE is insufficient. Electrical PPE requirements are governed by OSHA's electrical standards (29 CFR 1926 Subpart K) and the NFPA 70E standard for electrical safety in the workplace.

**Core electrical PPE for construction:**

- **Voltage-rated insulating rubber gloves** — classified from Class 00 (500V max) to Class 4 (36,000V max). Must be tested at regular intervals and before each use.
- **Leather protector gloves** — worn over rubber insulating gloves to protect them from mechanical damage.
- **Dielectric safety footwear** — ASTM F2413 EH-rated boots for protection against live circuits.
- **Arc flash rated clothing** — for work on or near energized electrical equipment. Arc flash clothing is rated in cal/cm² and must be matched to the incident energy at the work location based on an arc flash hazard analysis.
- **Class E hard hat** — for work near overhead power lines or electrical installations.
- **Face shield** — arc-rated face shield for protection against arc flash; minimum arc rating matched to the hazard analysis.

## PPE Procurement for Construction: What to Look for at Scale

For construction contractors, safety managers, and procurement professionals purchasing PPE for large workforces or multiple sites, the following considerations apply.

For a procurement-focused workflow covering RFQs, sizing, replacement stock, supplier comparison, and bulk packaging, use the [bulk construction PPE procurement guide](/blog/bulk-construction-ppe-procurement).

### Certification verification

Every item of PPE supplied to construction workers must carry the appropriate certification mark for its category. In the US: ANSI/ISEA, ASTM, NIOSH, or NFPA as applicable. In Europe: **CE marking** under **EU Regulation 2016/425**, with the relevant EN standard number.

**Understanding CE marking for construction PPE:**

EU Regulation 2016/425 classifies PPE into three categories based on risk level:
- **Category I** — minor risks (self-certified by manufacturer; not applicable to most construction PPE)
- **Category II** — intermediate risks (requires third-party EU Type-Examination by a Notified Body). Most construction PPE falls here: hard hats, safety glasses, work gloves, hearing protectors, hi-vis clothing, most safety footwear.
- **Category III** — irreversible or lethal risks (requires the highest level of third-party certification plus ongoing production surveillance). **Fall protection equipment (harnesses, lanyards, SRLs), respiratory protection for hazardous atmospheres, and electrical insulating gloves** all fall into Category III.

A CE mark on Category III PPE should always be accompanied by a 4-digit Notified Body number (e.g., CE 0086 for BSI, CE 0123 for DEKRA). Always verify the Notified Body number matches a legitimate EU-approved body — counterfeit CE-marked PPE, particularly fall protection harnesses and respirators, has been documented in global markets.

**For global procurement teams:** PPE certified to both NIOSH/ANSI (US) and CE/EN (EU) standards provides maximum flexibility for multinational construction operations and export markets. Always request the EU Declaration of Conformity (DoC) and test certificates from manufacturers alongside NIOSH TC approval numbers for respirators.

### Sizing and the 2025 OSHA requirement

The January 2025 update to 29 CFR 1926.95 makes proper fit a legal requirement, not just a best practice. When ordering PPE in bulk for a construction workforce, this means ordering across the full size range rather than defaulting to a single "standard" size. Hard hats, harnesses, gloves, safety glasses, and protective footwear must all be available in sizes that fit every worker on the site.

### Bulk ordering and replacement planning

Construction PPE has defined service lives that must be managed systematically:

- Hard hat shells: typically 5 years from manufacture date; suspension: 1–2 years
- Full-body harnesses: remove from service after any fall arrest; inspect annually at minimum; maximum service life per manufacturer (typically 10 years)
- Safety glasses: replace when lenses are scratched to the point of reduced visibility or when frames are deformed
- Disposable respirators: single use or limited reuse depending on model
- Hearing protection: replace foam earplugs when soiled or deformed; replace earmuff cushions when cracked or hardened

A documented replacement schedule aligned with manufacturer recommendations ensures PPE remains compliant and effective throughout the duration of a project.

### Training and compliance

Providing PPE is a legal obligation. Ensuring it is worn correctly is equally important and equally required. OSHA mandates training for all workers who use PPE, covering: when PPE is required, what PPE is required, how to put on, take off, and adjust it, the limitations of the PPE, and the proper care, maintenance, and disposal.

Workers who are not trained — or who are trained but choose not to wear their PPE correctly — represent a compliance gap and a genuine safety risk. Site inductions, toolbox talks, supervisor enforcement, and documented training records are all part of an effective PPE compliance program.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Who is responsible for providing PPE on a construction site?**  
Under 29 CFR 1926.95, the employer is responsible for providing PPE at no cost to employees in most circumstances. Exceptions include non-specialty safety-toe footwear and non-specialty prescription safety eyewear when the employer permits these items to be worn off the job site. General contractors are also responsible for ensuring subcontractor workers on their sites are equipped with appropriate PPE.

**What is the most frequently cited OSHA violation in construction?**  
Fall protection (29 CFR 1926.501) has remained OSHA's most frequently cited standard across all industries, including construction exposure, and continues to dominate enforcement attention on active jobsites.

**Does PPE have to fit properly under OSHA rules?**  
Yes. Effective January 13, 2025, 29 CFR 1926.95(c) explicitly requires that all construction PPE be "selected to ensure that it properly fits each affected employee." Employers must provide PPE in appropriate sizes and cannot simply adjust oversized equipment to fit.

**Can workers use their own PPE instead of employer-provided equipment?**  
Yes, if the employer permits it. However, the employer must verify that the employee-provided PPE meets the applicable safety standard and fits the worker correctly. The employer is not required to reimburse the cost if the worker chooses to use their own equipment.

**How often should PPE be inspected on a construction site?**  
Inspection frequency depends on the PPE type. Fall protection equipment must be inspected before each use. Hard hats should be inspected daily for cracks, dents, and penetration marks. Electrical insulating gloves require periodic electrical testing. Respiratory protection requires inspection before each use and regular filter replacement. All PPE should be formally inspected at least annually by a competent person.

**What are the penalties for PPE violations in construction?**  
As of the federal adjustment effective January 15, 2025, OSHA penalties can reach $16,550 for serious violations and $165,514 for willful or repeated violations. Beyond financial penalties, OSHA may issue stop-work orders for sites with immediate danger conditions.
